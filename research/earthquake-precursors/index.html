Can you see an earthquake coming from orbit? We checked.

There is a large body of papers claiming satellites pick up warning signs days before big earthquakes: ionospheric disturbances, infrared "thermal anomalies", odd radiation readings. If true, this would be the most valuable signal in Earth observation. So we ran the test ourselves, on free public data, with the one safeguard most of those papers skip: a control window where no earthquake happened.

The setup

We took three well-studied events: Tohoku 2011 (M9.0), Gorkha, Nepal 2015 (M7.8), and Kahramanmaras, Turkey 2023 (M7.8). For each one we pulled four datasets over the 75 days around the quake, all free and anonymous to download:

  • Ionospheric electron content (TEC) from JPL global ionosphere maps, every 2 hours, interpolated to the epicenter. Anomaly rule: outside the median ±1.5×IQR of the previous 15 days, the standard detector in this literature.
  • Outgoing longwave radiation (OLR) from NOAA daily grids, scored against a 10-year same-calendar-day climatology. Anomaly rule: z > 2.
  • Ground skin temperature from NASA POWER, scored the same way against 2001–2024.
  • Kp geomagnetic index from GFZ, to mark solar-storm days, the main thing that moves the ionosphere and gets mistaken for a precursor.

Every threshold was fixed before we computed anything. Then the key step: each earthquake window got a twin, the identical 75-day window at the same location exactly one year earlier, run through exactly the same pipeline. If the detectors light up in a quiet year too, they are measuring weather and space weather, not earthquakes.

The results

Verdict: nothing here predicts an earthquake. The anomalies are real fluctuations, but they appear at the same rate when no earthquake follows. With fixed thresholds and honest controls, every "precursor" we reproduced dissolves into solar activity, weather, or base rate. This matches the systematic critiques of the precursor literature, and it is why decades of retrospective case studies have never produced a working prospective forecast.

Why we publish negative results

Because the alternative is selling things that do not work. Satellites are genuinely good at the other side of this problem: measuring where strain is accumulating, mapping ground deformation to millimeter precision, and delivering damage assessments within hours of an event. That is measurement, it holds up, and it is what we build on. Prediction from orbit does not hold up, so we do not offer it, and this page is the receipt.

Method notes: three events is a demonstration, so treat the statistics accordingly. Global ionosphere maps smooth over any hypothetical local feature at their 5°×2.5° resolution. The Tohoku temperature point sits offshore. Analysis code and data sources are a few hundred lines of Python against JPL, NOAA, NASA POWER and GFZ public archives; write us if you want the scripts.
