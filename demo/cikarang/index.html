Demo · Cikarang, Indonesia · live archive, null result

Eight data centers, two years, nothing moving

Cikarang carries most of Indonesia's new data center capacity, on the same alluvial plain that has made North Jakarta one of the fastest sinking cities on record. We processed 119 interferograms from the free Sentinel-1 archive, 61 passes between September 2024 and September 2026, and sampled the stack at the coordinates of eight data centers taken from PeeringDB and OpenStreetMap.

Every one of them is flat to within our own noise. This page is what a screening run looks like when the answer is no, and what it looked like for one day when we sampled the wrong 400 metres.

The correction · why coordinates come first

An estate centroid is not a building

The first version of this study sampled one point per industrial estate and reported that Jababeka was sinking 23 mm/yr faster than its neighbours, with the estate's data center tenant named beside the number. Kota Jababeka covers about 5,600 hectares. The point we used sits 6.2 km from the nearest verified data hall, on the flank of a regional subsidence lobe. The ground there really is moving. No data center is on it.

The middle button above puts that series back on the chart. It is the same pipeline, the same stack and the same inversion, sampled 6 km away, and it is a 23 mm/yr difference in the headline.

Where everything sits

Line of sight velocity across the scene, red moving away from the satellite. The small circles are the verified data centers: DCI H1 in MM2100 to the west, GTN in the middle, and six more in the GIIC cluster at Kota Deltamas to the south. The orange circle is the estate point, on the southern flank of the subsiding lobe that covers the north east of the scene. That lobe is regional groundwater drawdown, not anything built on top of it.

What a null result is worth

Reading this the way an operator would

Nothing here clears its own error bar, and that is the finding. Free radar over two years puts the ground under all eight halls inside a few mm/yr of each other and of zero. An operator who was about to commission a ground survey on a hunch now has an ordering: none of these eight, and a lobe 6 km north east that would matter a great deal to anyone building in it.

What it is not is a certificate. Our own rates were off by 54 mm/yr RMS against GNSS on tropical ground in Puerto Rico over a six-month window, which spread across two years is still of order 20 mm/yr on an absolute rate. Nothing in the table clears that bar either, which is why this is read as a null and not as eight precise measurements.
